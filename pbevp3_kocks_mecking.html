PB-EVP3 · Physics-based elastoviscoplastic models

Kocks–Mecking (1976, 1981) and Estrin–Mecking (1984)

A one-internal-variable model: the dislocation density ρ. Dislocations are stored by athermal processes and annihilated by dynamic recovery, which depends on the strain rate and the temperature. The flow stress follows from ρ through the Taylor relation. Estrin and Mecking added the storage at grain boundaries, which makes fine-grained materials harden faster.

Constitutive equations

σ = σ0 + M α μ(T) b √ρ · (ε̇p/ε̇0)1/m
dρ/dεp = M [ 1/(b d) + k1 √ρ − k2(ε̇p, T) ρ ],    k2 = k20 (ε̇p/ε̇0)−1/n
1/m = k T / (A1 μ b³),    1/n = k T / (A2 μ b³)
  • M = 3.06 is the Taylor factor of an FCC polycrystal, α ≈ 0.3 the dislocation interaction constant, b = 0.255 nm the Burgers vector, μ(T) the shear modulus of copper (as in PB-EVP2).
  • k1√ρ: storage. A dislocation travels a distance proportional to the forest spacing 1/√ρ before it is stopped. 1/(b d): storage at grain boundaries, with the grain size d (Estrin–Mecking). k2 ρ: dynamic recovery by cross-slip and annihilation, a thermally activated process. k2 is larger at low rate and high temperature.
  • 1/m is the instantaneous rate sensitivity (small, about 0.005 at room temperature). 1/n is the rate sensitivity of the recovery, larger, which makes the saturation stress depend on the rate.
  • Without the grain size term, the stage III hardening rate is linear in the stress: θ = θII(1 − σ/σs), with the saturation stress σs ∝ k1/k2. This is the Voce law, derived from the dislocation density.

Physical basis

Kocks (1976) proposed that the hardening of FCC metals is the balance between storage and recovery of dislocations, with a single structure variable. Mecking and Kocks (1981) related it to the kinetics of flow, and Estrin and Mecking (1984) wrote the unified form used here. Because the state is ρ, a change of rate or temperature has an instant effect through 1/m, and a delayed effect through k2, which needs some strain to change ρ. The same equations give work hardening, steady-state creep and the strain-rate history effect.

What to look for

Rate jump. The stress jumps up by a small instantaneous amount, then hardens towards the fast monotonic curve over a few percent of strain. After the jump back, it stays above the slow curve and relaxes towards it. The tiles show the difference with the monotonic curves.

Kocks–Mecking plot. θ against σ is a straight line in stage III. The fast-rate line ends at a higher stress (less recovery). With grain size storage (Estrin–Mecking) the line starts higher and is curved at low stress.

Temperatures. The saturation stress falls strongly with the temperature: recovery is thermally activated. The initial yield stress hardly changes.

Creep. At constant stress, ρ grows until storage and recovery balance at the creep rate: primary creep with a decreasing rate, then steady-state creep. The steady creep rate is very sensitive to the stress.
