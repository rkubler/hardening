EVP5 · Elastoviscoplastic (rate-dependent) models

Johnson–Cook (1983)

The standard model for high strain rates (impact, machining, crash). The flow stress is a product of three factors: strain hardening, a logarithmic rate term and thermal softening. Written here as a viscoplastic flow rule, so it can also be run in relaxation, where its limits show.

Constitutive equations

σ = (A + B pn) (1 + C ln(ṗ / ε̇0)) (1 − T*m),   T* = (T − Tref)/(Tmelt − Tref)
inverted:   ṗ = ε̇0 exp[ (|σ|/σs(p) − 1) / C ] for |σ| > σs(p),   σs(p) = (A + B pn)(1 − T*m)
  • A, B, n: yield stress and strain hardening (Ludwik form) at the reference rate ε̇0 and room temperature.
  • C: rate sensitivity. The flow stress rises by a factor 1 + C ln 10 per decade of strain rate: about 3 % per decade for C = 0.014.
  • T*, m: homologous temperature and thermal softening exponent. Here T* is a fixed input (isothermal test); in impact simulations it is raised by the adiabatic heating of plastic work.
  • The original equation gives σ for a known rate. For a material point, it is inverted as above. Below the reference rate the logarithm would give a lower stress than σs; most codes, like this page, cut it off: no plastic flow while |σ| ≤ σs(p), so below ε̇0 the response is rate independent.

What to look for

Tension at several rates. Rates below ε̇0 give the same curve (the cut-off); above it the curves rise by the same factor at every strain: rate and hardening are uncoupled (multiplicative). The second plot is a straight line above ε̇0, of slope C·σs ln 10 per decade, and flat below. The second curve uses the temperature T* you set: the whole surface is scaled by 1 − T*m.

Relaxation. The stress relaxes quickly down to σs(p) and stops there: the model has a yield surface and no creep below it.

Default parameters are the classic values for 4340 steel (A = 792 MPa, B = 510 MPa, n = 0.26, C = 0.014, m = 1.03, ε̇0 = 1 /s). Isothermal: no adiabatic heating.
